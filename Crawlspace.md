**Spiders** (order [Araneae](#t)) are air-breathing [arthropods](#t) with eight legs, fanged [chelicerae](#t) that in most species deliver [venom](#t), and abdominal [spinnerets](#t) that extrude [silk](#t). They are the largest order of [arachnids](#t), with more than 52,000 described species, and they live on every continent except Antarctica.

A spider's body has two main parts, the [cephalothorax](#t) and the [abdomen](#t), joined by a narrow waist called the [pedicel](#t). Most species have eight eyes, though their arrangement and sharpness vary widely between families. Spiders have no antennae. Fine sensory hairs called [trichobothria](#t) pick up air currents and vibrations instead.

## Silk

Every spider makes silk, and many make several kinds from separate glands. [Orb-weavers](#t) can produce up to seven types, each suited to a job: dragline for safety lines and web frames, a sticky capture spiral, egg-sac wrapping, and swathing silk for prey. Dragline silk combines strength and stretch so well that, weight for weight, it is tougher than high-grade steel.

Not all silk goes into webs. Many spiderlings disperse by [ballooning](#t), letting out threads that catch the wind and carry them for kilometres. The [diving bell spider](#t) builds a silk bubble underwater and fills it with air carried down from the surface.

## Hunting

Roughly half of all spider species catch prey without a web. [Jumping spiders](#t) use large forward-facing principal eyes to judge distance well enough to leap onto prey several body lengths away. [Wolf spiders](#t) run prey down on the ground, and [crab spiders](#t) wait inside flowers for pollinators. One jumping spider, *Bagheera kiplingi*, feeds mostly on plant tissue from acacia trees, which is rare for a predator.
