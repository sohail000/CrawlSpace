Spiders (order Araneae) are air-breathing arthropods with eight legs, fanged chelicerae that in most species deliver venom, and abdominal spinnerets that extrude silk. They are the largest order of arachnids, with more than 52,000 described species, and they live on every continent except Antarctica.

A spider's body has two main parts, the cephalothorax and the abdomen, joined by a narrow waist called the pedicel. Most species have eight eyes, though their arrangement and sharpness vary widely between families. Spiders have no antennae. Fine sensory hairs called trichobothria pick up air currents and vibrations instead.

Silk

Every spider makes silk, and many make several kinds from separate glands. Orb-weavers can produce up to seven types, each suited to a job: dragline for safety lines and web frames, a sticky capture spiral, egg-sac wrapping, and swathing silk for prey. Dragline silk combines strength and stretch so well that, weight for weight, it is tougher than high-grade steel.

Not all silk goes into webs. Many spiderlings disperse by ballooning, letting out threads that catch the wind and carry them for kilometres. The diving bell spider builds a silk bubble underwater and fills it with air carried down from the surface.

Hunting

Roughly half of all spider species catch prey without a web. Jumping spiders use large forward-facing principal eyes to judge distance well enough to leap onto prey several body lengths away. Wolf spiders run prey down on the ground, and crab spiders wait inside flowers for pollinators. One jumping spider, Bagheera kiplingi, feeds mostly on plant tissue from acacia trees, which is rare for a predator.
